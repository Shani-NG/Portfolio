3\. Executive Summary

Monitoring and Product Intelligence transformed fragmented feedback into a product-learning mechanism. Before the initiative, product decisions depended heavily on field impressions, commander summaries, evaluation notes, and Excel reports. Those sources were valuable but often delayed, filtered, subjective, and disconnected from actual user behavior.

The initiative created a behavioral analytics and measurement layer that connected scenario mapping, workflow telemetry, UX benchmarking, field context, release evaluation, adoption learning, and product prioritization. Its purpose was not to create dashboards. Its purpose was to help the organization distinguish product friction from training, awareness, configuration, technical, or local operational issues.

The work established a stronger evidence base for product and engineering decisions while remaining within strict confidentiality constraints.

4\. Project Context

System environment  
The initiative supported complex operational platforms used by more than 100 users across multiple systems. Users included operators and officers working under pressure with limited time for structured feedback.

Legacy evaluation model  
Feedback was often consolidated by representatives into generic reports. This created distance between actual behavior, the interpretation of that behavior, and the resulting product decision.

Strategic problem  
The organization lacked reliable visibility into whether users discovered new capabilities, completed workflows, abandoned them, repurposed them, misunderstood them, or simply did not know they existed.

Core product question  
How can the organization learn from actual user behavior without replacing qualitative context or relying only on post-event reporting?

5\. Users and Stakeholders

Operational users  
Operators and officers using complex systems under pressure. Their time for formal interviews or detailed reporting is limited.

Field representatives and commanders  
Stakeholders who gather, summarize, and communicate field feedback. Their reports are useful but may introduce interpretation, hierarchy bias, or aggregation loss.

Product and UX teams  
Teams responsible for deciding whether a reported problem requires redesign, prioritization, onboarding, training, configuration, or further investigation.

R\&D and engineering  
Teams needing evidence about feature adoption, workflow completion, failure patterns, and the value of released capabilities.

Field observers  
Non-UX personnel who may conduct evaluations when the UX team cannot be present.

7\. Problem Definition

7.1 The feedback black box  
The organization had feedback, but it was difficult to determine what the product should learn from it.

7.2 Subjectivity and filtering  
Evaluations conducted by people without UX orientation could mix observation, interpretation, and recommendation, reducing the usefulness of the raw evidence.

7.3 Identification gaps  
Users could not always distinguish between a technical defect, missing functionality, system limitation, poor discoverability, insufficient training, or incorrect usage.

7.4 Hierarchy bias  
Senior voices or commander-level summaries could carry disproportionate weight compared with the recurring friction experienced by day-to-day operators.

7.5 R\&D visibility gap  
Product and engineering teams lacked reliable evidence about whether capabilities were discovered, adopted, bypassed, abandoned, or used differently from the intended design.

7.6 Adoption versus development  
Some “missing feature” requests referred to existing capabilities that were hidden, misunderstood, or poorly introduced.

7.7 Organizational friction  
Moving from feature-first discussion toward evidence-informed product learning required sustained advocacy, implementation effort, and cross-functional alignment.

8\. Strategic Reframing

The initiative was not framed as a dashboard project or a narrow analytics implementation. It was designed as a learning system.

The strategic shift  
\- From isolated field claims to behavioral evidence combined with operational context.  
\- From generic usage counts to measurable workflow intent.  
\- From feature requests to questions about discovery, completion, failure, abandonment, adoption, and training.  
\- From one-time launch assumptions to ongoing release evaluation.  
\- From debating opinions to categorizing the source of friction before choosing a response.

Core product questions  
\- Are users discovering the capability?  
\- Are they completing the intended workflow?  
\- Where do they fail, abandon, bypass, or repurpose the flow?  
\- What does the evidence indicate about product design, training, awareness, adoption, or technical quality?

9\. Scenario Mapping

Purpose  
Scenario mapping translated a broad concept such as “usage” into measurable product intent.

For each important capability or workflow, the model considered  
\- User type.  
\- Operational goal.  
\- Feature discovery and first use.  
\- Workflow start, completion, failure, bypass, and abandonment.  
\- Time to complete key actions.  
\- Repeated use after initial exposure.  
\- Behavioral differences between user groups.  
\- Adoption after release.  
\- Signals indicating training gaps versus product friction.  
\- Time elapsed since the capability reached the field.

Documented value  
The scenario kit gave product and engineering a shared structure for defining successful use before interpreting telemetry.

10\. Matomo as a Product-Evaluation Layer

Status  
Documented implementation foundation.

Matomo was selected as the infrastructure for the behavioral analytics layer after platform considerations including security and flexibility.

The implementation focused on complete workflows rather than page views or generic dashboard counts.

Relevant signals  
\- Task success.  
\- Time on task.  
\- Errors and failed attempts.  
\- Abandonment and drop-off.  
\- Adoption and repeated use.  
\- Discoverability.  
\- User-type patterns.  
\- Lightweight perception measures where appropriate.

Documented purpose  
Connect telemetry to release evaluation, prioritization, training, redesign, onboarding, configuration, and adoption decisions.

Guardrail  
Do not describe Matomo as the product. Do not claim that every possible workflow or product feature was measured unless specifically supported.

11\. Measurement Architecture

The intended evidence model combined behavioral telemetry with UX benchmarking and field context.

Evidence stack  
1\. Behavioral Data  
What users actually did in the system.

2\. Task Benchmark  
Whether a defined workflow could be completed and how efficiently.

3\. Perception Pulse  
A lightweight measure of perceived ease or usability when appropriate.

4\. Field Context  
Operational conditions, user role, release context, and qualitative explanation required to interpret the behavior.

Potential measurement methods  
\- Top-task success.  
\- Time on task.  
\- Single Ease Question.  
\- UMUX-Lite.  
\- System Usability Scale.  
\- Confidence intervals only when the sample, study design, and method support them.

KPI principle  
Prefer three to four meaningful KPIs per workflow over a large generic dashboard.

Implementation-status rule  
The source presents some measurement methods as intended or potential components of the architecture. Do not imply that every method was implemented in every workflow.

12\. The Field-Research Cookbook

The Cookbook was a UX-guidance manual created for non-UX field observers.

Purpose  
Help stakeholders collect less filtered and more useful evidence when the UX team could not be present.

Supported practices  
\- More consistent observation.  
\- Clearer separation between behavior, interpretation, and recommendation.  
\- Better documentation of workflow context.  
\- Reduced dependence on summary-only reporting.  
\- A stronger bridge between field evaluation and product decisions.

Documented value  
It expanded the quality and reach of field evidence without claiming that non-UX observers replaced professional UX research.

13\. Early Findings

The source documents that useful patterns became visible during the first week of monitoring.

13.1 Used, but wrong  
Some forms were used frequently but in unintended ways.

Interpretation  
The behavior indicated a real need while raising questions about workflow design, naming, defaults, or training.

13.2 Useful, but invisible  
Some newer forms with potential value were barely opened.

Interpretation  
The issue could be discoverability, awareness, onboarding, timing, or relevance—not necessarily missing functionality.

13.3 Patterns to presets  
Repeated behavior patterns were translated into role-based presets.

Documented value  
The presets supported faster setup and a more relevant starting point for different user types.

Guardrail  
The source states that setup time was reduced, but no quantified reduction is available. Describe this qualitatively unless verified measurements are added.

14\. Product-Learning and Decision Logic

The combined evidence enabled the team to ask more precise questions before prioritizing a response.

Decision categories  
\- Design problem.  
\- Technical problem.  
\- Training problem.  
\- Awareness or discoverability problem.  
\- Configuration or default-setting problem.  
\- Local operational condition.  
\- Systemic product issue.  
\- Truly missing capability.

Possible responses  
\- New development.  
\- Improved onboarding.  
\- Better defaults or role-based presets.  
\- Clearer naming.  
\- Workflow redesign.  
\- Training or communication.  
\- Technical repair.  
\- Additional research or measurement.

15\. Outcomes and Evidence Boundaries

Supported qualitative outcomes  
\- The initiative received stronger organizational attention and priority because useful patterns became visible quickly.  
\- It created a foundation for evidence-backed prioritization.  
\- It improved the organization’s ability to evaluate releases and distinguish adoption gaps from development gaps.  
\- It supported more confident product discussions by combining behavioral evidence with field context.  
\- It helped direct attention toward higher-impact opportunities.  
\- It established product learning as an ongoing strategic capability.

Claims requiring caution  
\- “More than 100 users” is platform reach, not a measured study sample.  
\- Do not claim statistical significance or representative research without sample and method details.  
\- Do not claim exact adoption percentages, success-rate improvements, or business ROI.  
\- Do not claim a quantified reduction in downtime, development cost, setup time, or training effort.  
\- Do not claim that every feature now includes a tracking plan without additional verification.  
\- Do not claim that every field report can be validated or refuted immediately.  
\- Do not claim proven causal transformation of organizational culture.

Safe outcome language  
Describe the mechanism established, the patterns observed, the decisions supported, and the qualitative shift in product discussion.
